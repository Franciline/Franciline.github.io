Decoding the Bottle

What can a wine’s price, origin, grapes, and taste profile tell us about its public rating?

Predicting wine ratings with machine learning, then explaining the patterns behind the predictions.

A DALAS course project by Amélie Chu and Ekaterina Bogush.

Read the report ↗View code ↗

Overview

Collected wine data from Vivino and SimpleWine to study how price, origin, grapes, and taste profiles relate to public ratings. The project explored dataset biases, imputed missing values, and compared regression and classification models to predict ratings and identify wines rated above average within their price range.

Approach & Results

  • Collected, harmonized, and explored wine data from two sources, investigating biases and imputing missing values.
  • Compared Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost models; CatBoost performed best among the tested configurations.
  • Achieved a held-out MAE of 0.163 stars and R² of 0.553 for public-rating prediction.
  • Achieved F1 of 0.684 and 66.7% accuracy for predicting above-average ratings within a price range.
  • Used SHAP to interpret model predictions, with price emerging as the strongest driver of predicted ratings.

From data to predictions

  1. Collect and prepare

    Harmonized data from two sources, normalized prices to USD and a 750 ml bottle, explored missingness, and imputed taste attributes using wine-type groupings. Inputs included origin, vintage, winery, grapes, food pairings, alcohol, and taste characteristics.

  2. Compare models

    Evaluated Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost. The report uses an 80/20 train-test split stratified by country and five-fold cross-validation on the training data to compare models.

  3. Account for price

    For classification, adjusted ratings relative to the mean within price bins and excluded price from the model inputs. The resulting target distinguishes wines rated above or below their price-group average.

  4. Explain predictions

    Used feature importance and SHAP to examine which variables influence predictions and whether their contributions increase or decrease a predicted rating.

The regression model captures part of the variation in ratings, with an average absolute error of approximately 0.16 stars. Classification is a harder question: which wines receive better ratings than their peers at similar prices?

What drives the predictions?

Price was a major driver of the rating model. The SHAP analysis also highlighted vintage, alcohol, and drinking window. Higher prices generally contributed to higher predicted ratings, but this association does not establish that a higher price causes better wine quality.

Limits and lessons

  • Ratings reflect user preferences, expectations, and the dataset’s coverage. They are not an objective quality standard.
  • The report notes weaker predictions for rare low and very high ratings: the model tends to pull these extremes toward more common values.
  • Missing taste attributes required approximate imputation. Sparse groups limited how finely the imputation could account for origin.
  • Price may reflect expectations or unobserved quality factors. Removing it from classification inputs does not eliminate every source of bias.

The project connects data preparation, predictive modeling, and interpretation, while showing why a useful prediction still needs careful explanation.
